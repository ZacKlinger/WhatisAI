Almost every claim in AI research rests on a number: a benchmark score, a growth rate, an effect in an experiment. Numbers are never as solid as they look in a headline. Every measurement carries noise, every chart makes choices about its axes, and every comparison can be fair or unfair. This module gives you the statistical instincts to read those numbers like a researcher. It may be the most practically useful module on the path.

## Every score is an estimate

Suppose a model scores 74% on a benchmark of 200 questions. Is its "true" accuracy 74%? Not necessarily. The benchmark is a *sample* of the questions the model might face. A different 200 questions of the same kind might yield 70% or 78%.

The **standard error** measures this wobble. For an accuracy *p* measured on *n* questions:

<Eq>standard error = √( <i>p</i> (1 − <i>p</i>) / <i>n</i> )</Eq>

For *p* = 0.74 and *n* = 200, that is about 0.031, or 3.1 percentage points. A **95% confidence interval** extends about two standard errors in each direction: roughly 68% to 80%. The lesson is immediate: two models scoring 72% and 74% on this benchmark are statistically indistinguishable.

Explore how this works. Set two models' true accuracies close together and watch how often the worse one ties or wins on a small benchmark. Then raise the number of questions.

## Significance and p-values

A result is **statistically significant** when it would be unlikely to occur by chance if there were no real effect. The **p-value** quantifies this: it is the probability of seeing a difference at least as large as the one observed, assuming there is truly no difference. By convention, p below 0.05 is called significant.

Three cautions keep you from being misled:

1. **A p-value is not the probability the hypothesis is true.** p = 0.03 does not mean "97% chance the effect is real."
2. **Significant does not mean large.** With enough data, trivially small effects become significant. Always ask about the **effect size**: how big is the difference, in units that matter?
3. **Not significant does not mean no effect.** A small study may simply lack the data to detect a real difference.

## How results get distorted

Many misleading results come not from fraud but from the quiet arithmetic of selection.

**Multiple comparisons.** Test twenty different prompts and one will likely look "significantly" better by chance alone at the 5% level. If a paper tried many variants and reports the best, its result is inflated.

**Cherry-picked examples.** A paper showing five impressive outputs tells you what the model *can* do, not what it *usually* does. Look for systematic evaluation over many examples.

**Benchmark contamination.** If test questions leaked into training data, high scores may reflect memory, not ability. You will study this in Track V.

**Unfair baselines.** A new method compared against an old, poorly tuned competitor will look better than it is.

## Correlation is not causation

Two things moving together do not prove one causes the other. When you read that "occupations more exposed to AI saw slower job growth," ask: could something else explain both? Perhaps exposed occupations were already declining, or were hit differently by interest rates. Economists address this with careful designs, such as comparing similar groups before and after a change (a *difference-in-differences*) or running randomized experiments. Track VII teaches you to read those designs.

## Logarithmic scales

AI quantities span enormous ranges. Training compute has grown by more than a billion times since 2012. On an ordinary axis, everything before the last few years would be squashed flat against zero. So researchers use **logarithmic scales**, where each equal step on the axis *multiplies* the value by the same factor, usually 10.

On a log axis, 1, 10, 100, and 1,000 are equally spaced. A change from 10<sup>20</sup> to 10<sup>21</sup> covers the same distance as 10<sup>24</sup> to 10<sup>25</sup>, even though the second jump is ten thousand times larger in absolute terms.

## Exponentials and doubling times

**Exponential growth** means growth by a constant factor per period. It appears as a straight line on a log-scale y-axis. A useful way to grasp it is the **doubling time**: if something doubles every seven months, then in about two years it grows more than tenfold, and in five years more than three hundredfold.

A quick rule of thumb: to find a doubling time from a growth rate, divide 70 by the percentage growth per period. Seven percent per year doubles in about ten years.

## Power laws

A **power law** has the form *y* = *a* · *x*<sup>*k*</sup>. Its signature is a straight line when *both* axes are logarithmic. Power laws are central to modern AI because of **scaling laws**: in 2020, OpenAI researchers found that a language model's loss falls as a power law in its size, its data, and its compute, holding across more than seven orders of magnitude. A straight line on a log-log plot, extending across years of progress, is one of the most important images in the field.

## Reading the corpus

| When a document says | It means |
| --- | --- |
| "± 1.2" or error bars | Usually one standard error, or a 95% interval; check which |
| "95% CI [68, 80]" | A 95% confidence interval from 68 to 80 |
| "p &lt; 0.05" | Statistically significant at the conventional threshold |
| "n = 200" | The sample size: here, 200 questions, participants, or runs |
| "log-log plot" | Both axes logarithmic; straight lines indicate power laws |
| "orders of magnitude" | Factors of ten; "three orders of magnitude" means 1,000 times |
| "pass@k" | The probability that at least one of k attempts succeeds |
