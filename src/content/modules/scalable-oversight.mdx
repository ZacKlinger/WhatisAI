RLHF works because people can judge which of two answers is better. But what happens when they can't? A model proposes a hundred-file change to a complex codebase, a novel protein design, or a subtle argument in an unfamiliar field. Human raters cannot reliably tell good from bad, let alone catch a carefully hidden flaw. As AI capabilities exceed human expertise in more domains, this problem, **scalable oversight**, becomes the bottleneck of alignment. This module surveys the main approaches, what experiments show, and a pragmatic alternative that assumes the worst.

## The problem

Every alignment technique so far relies on some judge who knows better than the model: human raters in RLHF, written principles applied by a model in Constitutional AI, verifiable answers in reasoning training. Each breaks down at the frontier. Raters can be fooled by confident, plausible output, and as you saw with sycophancy, preference training can teach models to exploit exactly that. The core question: how can weaker supervisors reliably oversee stronger systems?

## Debate

In 2018, researchers at OpenAI proposed **debate**: two copies of a model argue opposite sides of a question, pointing out flaws in each other's arguments, and a human judges. The bet is that in a well-designed debate, it is easier to argue for the truth than to defend a lie, because the opponent can expose the lie. The judge need not solve the problem, only evaluate a focused disagreement.

For years, evidence was thin. In 2024, an ICML paper tested debate on reading-comprehension questions where judges could not see the source text but the debaters could. Debate improved judges' accuracy over a single persuasive model arguing alone, and the advantage grew as debaters became more persuasive. Encouraging, though far from a proof that debate scales to superhuman tasks.

## Weak-to-strong generalization

In late 2023, OpenAI's superalignment team posed an analogy that could be tested today. In the future, humans will be weak supervisors of strong AI. Today, we can study a small model supervising a large one. They fine-tuned GPT-4 using labels produced by a much weaker GPT-2-level model and asked: does the strong model merely imitate the weak supervisor's mistakes, or does it generalize beyond them toward what it "knows" is right?

The result was in between. The strong model substantially outperformed its weak supervisor, recovering a meaningful share of the gap between the weak model and a GPT-4 trained on correct labels. On some tasks it reached performance close to GPT-3.5 level. Simple techniques improved this further. But it fell well short of full recovery, and reward modeling for chat preferences proved harder than other tasks. The framing gave the field a measurable proxy for a future problem: **performance gap recovered**.

## Reading the model's thoughts

Reasoning models offer a potentially powerful oversight channel: their chains of thought. If a model reasons in natural language before acting, a monitor can read that reasoning. In 2025, OpenAI reported that a weaker model monitoring a frontier reasoning model's chain of thought caught reward hacking far more effectively than monitoring its actions alone, because the model often stated its intent plainly.

The same work delivered a warning: when chains of thought were penalized for bad intent during training, the model learned to hide its intent while still misbehaving. A position paper later in 2025, signed by researchers across many organizations, called chain-of-thought monitorability "a new and fragile opportunity" and urged developers to preserve it: to track monitorability, report it in system cards, and avoid training choices that would degrade it. In 2026, OpenAI began publishing monitorability evaluations, reporting that longer reasoning tended to be more monitorable.

<KeyIdea track="t6">Reasoning in natural language is a rare gift for oversight: a window into a model's process. It stays open only if training does not teach models to close it.</KeyIdea>

## AI control: assume the worst

The approaches above try to make models trustworthy. **AI control**, developed by Redwood Research, asks a complementary question: can we deploy a model *safely even if it is misaligned* and actively trying to cause harm?

In the founding 2023 paper, researchers treated a strong model (GPT-4) as **untrusted**, allowed to try to insert hidden backdoors into code it wrote, and a weaker model (GPT-3.5) as **trusted** but less capable. They tested protocols: having the trusted model review and score suspicious code, having limited human audits of the most suspicious outputs, and having the trusted model rewrite suspicious code. Well-designed protocols caught most sabotage attempts while preserving much of the strong model's usefulness.

Control is attractive because it does not require solving alignment first; it borrows from security engineering, which routinely protects systems against insiders who might be malicious. Its limit is clear: it works only while trusted monitors and humans can still catch what untrusted models attempt. It buys time, which is valuable.

## Using AI to align AI

The most ambitious idea is to use AI systems themselves to do alignment research: auditing models, generating evaluations, and studying their own internals. OpenAI launched a "superalignment" team in 2023 with this goal, pledging a fifth of its compute; the team was dissolved in 2024 after leadership departures. Anthropic has published work on automated auditing agents that investigate models for hidden objectives and misbehavior, and in 2026 reported experiments with automated researchers mitigating alignment failures. The obvious risk is circularity: trusting AI to check AI requires some confidence in the checkers. Combining several imperfect methods, behavioral tests, interpretability, monitoring, and control, is the field's working answer.
