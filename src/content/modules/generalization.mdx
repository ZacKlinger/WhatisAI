A student who memorizes last year's exam answers can score perfectly on last year's exam and fail this year's. Machine learning faces the same temptation at an enormous scale. A model with billions of parameters could, in principle, memorize its entire training set. The question that defines the field is how to get a model that has *learned* rather than *memorized*: one that performs well on data it has never seen.

## Splitting the data

The first defense is procedural. Data is divided into separate sets:

- **Training set:** the examples the model learns from.
- **Validation set:** held-out examples used during development to compare settings and to notice when the model starts to overfit.
- **Test set:** held-out examples used once, at the end, to report how well the model generalizes.

If information from the test set leaks into training or into design decisions, the reported score becomes optimistic. In large language models, where training data is scraped from the web, this leakage is a serious and recurring problem called **contamination**. You will study it in the benchmarks module.

## Underfitting and overfitting

Fit a curve to noisy data and two failures are possible. Try it below. Slide the model's complexity from a straight line to a wildly flexible curve.

With too little flexibility, the model **underfits**: it misses the real pattern, and errors are high everywhere. With too much, it **overfits**: it threads every training point, including the noise, and swings wildly between them, so error on new data rises even as training error falls toward zero. The sweet spot captures the pattern without the noise.

The classic signature appears in learning curves over training time too. Training loss keeps falling; validation loss falls, bottoms out, and begins to rise.

## Regularization

**Regularization** is the family of techniques that discourage overfitting:

- **Weight decay** penalizes large parameter values, nudging the model toward simpler functions. (You saw this as the regularization slider above.)
- **Dropout** randomly switches off neurons during training, so the network cannot rely on any single fragile pathway.
- **Data augmentation** creates varied copies of training examples, such as flipped or cropped images.
- **Early stopping** halts training when validation loss stops improving.
- **More data** is the most powerful regularizer of all: it is harder to memorize a billion examples than a thousand.

## The puzzle of huge models

Classical statistics taught a clear lesson: the more parameters a model has relative to its data, the more it will overfit. By that logic, modern neural networks should be catastrophic. Many have more parameters than training examples, and in 2016 researchers showed that standard image networks could perfectly memorize datasets whose labels had been *randomly shuffled*, meaning they had enough capacity to fit pure noise. Yet trained on real labels, the same networks generalized well. Something about the classical picture was incomplete.

### Double descent

In 2019, researchers documented **double descent**. As model size increases, test error first follows the classical U-shape: falling, then rising as the model becomes just barely able to fit the training data. But beyond that point, as the model keeps growing, test error falls *again*, often to its lowest values. OpenAI researchers showed the same pattern in modern deep networks, across model size and training time.

One intuition: when a model is just barely large enough to fit the data, there is essentially one way to do it, and it is contorted. When a model is vastly larger, there are many ways to fit the data, and training with gradient descent tends to find smooth, simple ones.

### Grokking

In 2022, OpenAI researchers reported an even stranger phenomenon. Small networks trained on simple arithmetic tasks first memorized the training examples, with validation performance near chance. Then, after training far longer than usual, validation accuracy suddenly jumped to near perfect. The network had "grokked" the underlying rule long after memorizing. Later interpretability work showed such networks gradually building a general algorithm alongside the memorized lookup table, until the general algorithm won out.

<KeyIdea track="t2">At modern scale, bigger models and more training often generalize better, not worse. This empirical surprise is part of why the field bet so heavily on scale.</KeyIdea>

## When generalization fails: distribution shift

Everything above assumes new data comes from the same distribution as the training data. In deployment, it often does not. A medical model trained at one hospital may stumble at another with different scanners. A language model trained on text up to a cutoff date knows nothing of events after it. This **distribution shift** is a major source of real-world AI failures, and a central concern of safety research: we need models that behave well not just on familiar inputs but on the unfamiliar situations they will inevitably meet.

<Callout type="caution" title="A deeper version of the problem">
A model can generalize its *capabilities* to new situations while generalizing its *goals* in unintended ways. Safety researchers call this goal misgeneralization: the model competently pursues the wrong objective outside its training distribution. You will meet it in the alignment track.
</Callout>

| When a document says | It means |
| --- | --- |
| "held-out" | Data not used in training |
| "in-distribution" / "out-of-distribution (OOD)" | Similar to the training data / different from it |
