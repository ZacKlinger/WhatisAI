Training happens once. Inference happens billions of times a day. Every message to an AI assistant, every line of code completed, every agent step is an act of inference: running a trained model forward to produce output. Its mechanics determine how fast models respond, how much they cost, and how much energy they use. They also explain features you see every day, like text streaming onto the screen word by word.

## One token at a time

A language model produces a distribution over the next token. To generate a response, inference runs a loop:

1. Feed in the context (the prompt so far).
2. Compute the distribution over the next token.
3. Choose a token according to a **decoding strategy**.
4. Append it to the context and go back to step 1.

This is **autoregressive decoding**. Try it with a tiny model trained in your browser:

The tiny model only knows which words followed each pair of words in its training text, so its output wanders. A transformer considers the entire context through attention and is vastly more capable, but the loop is identical. The model never plans the whole sentence at once in its output, though, as interpretability research has found, it may represent plans internally.

## Decoding strategies

How should step 3 choose?

- **Greedy decoding** always takes the most probable token. It is deterministic and tends toward repetitive, flat text.
- **Temperature sampling** samples from the distribution after dividing logits by a temperature, trading predictability for variety.
- **Top-k** samples only from the k most probable tokens. **Top-p** (nucleus sampling) samples from the smallest set whose probabilities sum to p, adapting to how confident the model is.

A 2020 study showed why pure greedy or beam search produces degenerate, repetitive text, and introduced nucleus sampling, now standard. When an API exposes "temperature" and "top_p" settings, these are the knobs.

## Prefill and decode

Inference has two phases with very different characters.

**Prefill** processes the entire prompt at once. All prompt tokens can go through the transformer in parallel, so this phase is compute-heavy but efficient. It determines **time to first token**.

**Decode** generates output tokens one by one. Each new token requires a full pass through the model, but only for one position. The chip must read all the model's parameters from memory to produce each token, so this phase is limited by **memory bandwidth**, not arithmetic. It determines how fast text streams.

This asymmetry is why providers price output tokens higher than input tokens, often several times higher.

## The KV cache

When generating token 501, attention needs the keys and values of tokens 1 through 500. Recomputing them every step would be wasteful, so they are stored: the **KV cache**. It makes each decoding step fast, but it consumes memory that grows with context length and with the number of simultaneous users. For long contexts, the KV cache can exceed the size of the model itself. Much inference engineering, from grouped-query attention to memory-paging systems, exists to shrink or manage it.

## Making inference cheaper

Inference costs have fallen dramatically: the price of reaching a given level of capability has dropped by orders of magnitude since 2022. Techniques include:

- **Quantization.** Storing weights in 8 or 4 bits instead of 16 halves or quarters memory needs and speeds up decoding, usually with modest quality loss.
- **Distillation.** Training a small model to imitate a large one. Many fast, cheap models are distilled from frontier models.
- **Batching.** Serving many users at once, so each read of the weights from memory serves many requests.
- **Speculative decoding.** A small, fast model drafts several tokens; the large model checks them all in one parallel pass, accepting the ones it agrees with. Output quality is unchanged; speed can double or better.
- **Mixture of experts.** Activating only part of the model per token, as you saw in the transformer module.

<Callout type="economy" title="Labor market lens">
Falling inference costs change which tasks are economical to automate. A task that costs a dollar of compute may not be worth automating; the same task at a tenth of a cent may be. When you read projections of AI's economic impact, check what they assume about inference prices, which have repeatedly fallen faster than forecast.
</Callout>

## Inference compute as a new axis

Reasoning models, which you will study in Track IV, generate long internal chains of thought before answering, sometimes tens of thousands of tokens per question. That shifts a growing share of computation from training to inference. A model can now become more capable on a hard problem simply by being allowed to think longer, at higher cost. Policymakers who designed rules around training compute are now grappling with this second axis.

<KeyIdea track="t3">Inference is a loop of predict, choose, append. Its cost is dominated by reading the model's weights for every generated token, which is why output is pricier than input and why so much engineering targets memory.</KeyIdea>
