In the summer of 1955, four researchers asked for funding to spend two months at Dartmouth College. They believed that "every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it," and that "a significant advance can be made" in a summer. They named the project *artificial intelligence*. The ambition was right. The timeline was off by about seventy years.

The history of AI is worth knowing not for its trivia but for its pattern. Again and again, researchers tried to build intelligence by writing down what they knew, and again and again, methods that simply learned from more data with more computation overtook them.

## Rules versus learning

For its first thirty years, mainstream AI was **symbolic**. Researchers wrote programs that manipulated symbols according to rules: if the patient has a fever and a rash, consider measles. This approach produced real successes, from theorem provers to medical expert systems, and it shares one fatal property. The world is too large and too messy to write down. Every rule has exceptions, every exception has exceptions, and the systems shattered on inputs their authors had not anticipated.

**Machine learning** inverts the approach. Instead of writing the rules, you write a system that can adjust itself, show it many examples, and let it find the rules. For decades this was hampered by small datasets and slow computers. Then three things arrived together: the internet supplied data at unprecedented scale, graphics processors (GPUs) supplied cheap parallel computation, and a few algorithmic refinements made deep networks trainable.

<Callout type="history" title="The 2012 moment">
The ImageNet challenge asked systems to label photographs among a thousand categories. In 2012, AlexNet, a deep convolutional network trained on two consumer GPUs, achieved a top-5 error of about 15%, against roughly 26% for the next best entry. A gap that large, overnight, is rare in any science. Within a few years nearly every computer vision group had switched to deep learning.
</Callout>

## The bitter lesson

In 2019 the reinforcement learning pioneer Rich Sutton wrote a short essay with a long shadow. "The biggest lesson that can be read from 70 years of AI research," he argued, "is that general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin." Chess fell to search, not to encoded grandmaster wisdom. Speech recognition fell to statistics, not to linguistic rules. Vision fell to learned features, not to hand-designed edge detectors.

The lesson is bitter because researchers repeatedly invested in building their own understanding into systems, which helped in the short term and then plateaued, while methods that simply scaled with more compute kept improving.

<KeyIdea track="t0">The most reliable driver of AI progress has been scale: more data, more parameters, more computation, applied through general methods that can absorb them.</KeyIdea>

You will meet this idea formally in the scaling laws module. Training compute for frontier models has grown by roughly four to five times per year since around 2010, a pace that dwarfs the historical doubling of chip performance.

## From predicting text to an assistant

The modern era rests on a surprisingly simple training task: given some text, predict the next piece of it. A model that does this well across the internet must, in some sense, learn grammar, facts, reasoning patterns, and styles of argument, because all of these help predict what comes next.

**GPT-3** (2020) showed that a large enough next-word predictor could perform tasks it was never explicitly trained on, given just a few examples in its prompt. But a raw predictor is an odd collaborator: ask it a question and it might continue with five more questions, because that is what text on the internet often does.

**ChatGPT** (2022) was the same family of model after additional training to follow instructions and to produce answers people preferred, a technique called reinforcement learning from human feedback. You will study it in Track IV. The technical change was modest; the change in usefulness was enormous.

**Reasoning models** (2024 onward) are trained, with reinforcement learning on problems that have checkable answers, to produce long internal chains of thought before responding. They brought large gains in mathematics, science, and programming, and introduced a second scaling axis: spending more computation at the moment of answering, not only during training.

**Agents** (2025 onward) place these models in loops with tools: a code interpreter, a web browser, a computer's mouse and keyboard. The question shifted from "can it answer?" to "how long a task can it carry out on its own?"

## Why the history matters for reading the corpus

Three patterns from this history will recur in nearly every document you read.

**Hype cycles are real, and so is progress.** AI has endured two winters after promises outran results. Skepticism toward bold claims is therefore historically justified. But the deep learning era has also delivered, repeatedly, things experts had called decades away. Both "it's all hype" and "it changes everything tomorrow" have been reliably wrong. The skill is to weigh specific evidence.

**Capabilities often arrive before understanding.** The field learned to build systems that work well before it learned why they work. Interpretability research, which you will study in Track VI, is a late attempt to catch up.

**Scale changes the questions.** As systems grew more capable, debates moved from "can it recognize a cat?" to "can it help design a pathogen?" and "who is accountable when an agent acts?" The policy documents of the 2020s exist because of the scaling of the 2010s.
