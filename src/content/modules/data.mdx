A model is shaped by what it is shown. Architecture and algorithms get the headlines, but practitioners often say the most important ingredient in a frontier model is its data: what was collected, what was thrown away, and in what proportions it was mixed. Data also sits at the center of some of the field's hardest questions, from copyright lawsuits to whether AI will run out of human-written text.

## Where the learning signal comes from

Machine learning methods differ in where their "answers" come from.

**Supervised learning** uses examples labeled by people: photos tagged "cat" or "dog," emails marked "spam." Labels are expensive, which historically limited datasets to millions of examples. ImageNet, the dataset behind the 2012 breakthrough, required people to hand-label over a million images.

**Self-supervised learning** removes the bottleneck. The data provides its own answers. Hide the next word of a sentence and ask the model to predict it; the real text tells you whether it was right. Every sentence ever written becomes a training example. This is how language models are pretrained, and it is why they could scale to trillions of training tokens.

**Reinforcement learning** learns from rewards for actions: winning a game, solving a math problem, producing an answer a person prefers. It is central to post-training and to reasoning models, which you will study in Track IV.

## Building a web-scale dataset

Most pretraining data begins with the web. **Common Crawl**, a nonprofit archive, has collected billions of web pages since 2008. Raw web text is mostly unusable: navigation menus, spam, duplicated boilerplate, machine-generated junk. Turning it into training data involves a pipeline:

1. **Extraction:** pull readable text out of HTML.
2. **Language identification:** sort text by language.
3. **Quality filtering:** remove low-quality pages, using rules and often a classifier trained to recognize "educational" or well-written text.
4. **Deduplication:** remove repeated and near-duplicate documents. Google researchers showed in 2021 that deduplication makes models better and reduces how often they regurgitate training text verbatim.
5. **Safety and privacy filtering:** remove certain harmful content and personal information.

Web text is then blended with code, books, scientific papers, mathematics, and multilingual sources. The proportions, the **data mixture**, are among the most closely guarded secrets of frontier labs, because they strongly shape what a model is good at.

<Callout type="corpus" title="What labs disclose">
Frontier system cards typically describe training data in a sentence or two: "a mix of publicly available data, licensed data, and data generated by our models." Open efforts such as Hugging Face's FineWeb document their pipelines in detail. The EU AI Act now requires providers of general-purpose models to publish a summary of their training content using a template issued by the AI Office.
</Callout>

## Quality over quantity

How much does quality matter? In 2023, Microsoft researchers trained a small coding model, phi-1, largely on "textbook quality" data, including text generated by a larger model, and it rivaled much bigger models on coding benchmarks. The broader lesson, now widely replicated, is that carefully filtered data can be worth many times its volume in raw web text. When a lab announces a model that matches a predecessor at a fraction of the size, better data is usually part of the story.

## Is the world running out of text?

Scaling laws (Track III) say models improve predictably with more data. But the supply of high-quality human-written text is finite. In 2024, researchers at Epoch AI estimated the stock of quality public human text at roughly 300 trillion tokens, and projected that if training continued its trend, frontier models would fully use it sometime between 2026 and 2032.

The field has several responses:

- **Training for more epochs:** repeating data a few times costs little at first, though returns diminish.
- **New modalities:** images, video, and audio contain vast amounts of information.
- **Synthetic data:** text generated by models, especially where its correctness can be checked automatically, as with math and code.
- **Reinforcement learning:** generating training signal from interaction rather than from text, which became the major frontier of 2024 to 2026.

<Callout type="caution" title="The risk of eating your own tail">
A 2024 study in Nature showed that models trained repeatedly on the output of previous models can degrade, losing the rare and unusual parts of the original distribution: a phenomenon its authors called model collapse. Later work suggests the risk is manageable when synthetic data is mixed with real data and filtered, which is how labs use it. The debate continues.
</Callout>

## Data, law, and labor

Data is also where AI meets law and the economy most directly.

- **Copyright.** Authors, artists, and publishers have sued AI companies in many jurisdictions, arguing that training on their work without permission infringes copyright. In 2025, two US federal judges ruled that training on lawfully acquired books could qualify as fair use, while leaving other questions open; in one of those cases, claims over books downloaded from pirate libraries led to a settlement reported at $1.5 billion. Many companies have also signed licensing deals with publishers. The legal landscape remains unsettled and varies by country.
- **Transparency mandates.** The EU AI Act and California's AB 2013 require developers to disclose information about training data.
- **Data work.** Behind every model is human labor: annotators who label data, write demonstrations, and rate responses. This workforce, often contracted globally, is an essential and sometimes overlooked part of the AI economy.

<KeyIdea track="t2">A model can only learn the patterns present in its data. When you read a capability claim, ask what the model was trained on. When you read a safety claim, ask what it was not.</KeyIdea>
