Ask a person to multiply 347 by 28 in their head and answer instantly, and they will likely get it wrong. Give them paper, and they will get it right. The difference is not intelligence but working space: the chance to write down intermediate steps. Language models turned out to be similar. Letting them "think out loud" before answering made them dramatically better at math, logic, and code. Since 2024, training models to do this well has become the most important frontier in AI capability.

## Chain-of-thought prompting

In 2022, Google researchers showed that simply including worked examples with step-by-step reasoning in the prompt, or even just adding the phrase "Let's think step by step," substantially improved large models' performance on math word problems and logic puzzles. The model writes intermediate steps, a **chain of thought**, then the answer.

Why does this work? A transformer does a fixed amount of computation per token. A hard problem may need more computation than one forward pass provides. Writing out intermediate steps lets the model spend more passes on the problem, and lets each step build on explicit results instead of holding everything internally. The chain of thought is, quite literally, extra working memory and extra computation.

## More samples, better answers

A second simple idea: sample many independent chains of thought and take the majority answer. This is **self-consistency**. If each chain is right more often than any particular wrong answer, voting amplifies accuracy. Explore how the gain depends on single-attempt accuracy:

Self-consistency shows the core principle behind reasoning models: **more computation at answer time can buy more accuracy**. It also shows the limit: if a model is systematically wrong, more samples of the same mistake do not help.

## Training to reason: reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards

Prompting a model to reason helps. *Training* it to reason helps far more. The key ingredient is a reward that can be checked automatically:

- In **mathematics**, compare the final answer to the known solution.
- In **programming**, run the code against tests.
- In **logic puzzles**, verify the solution against the rules.

Such **verifiable rewards** are cheap, objective, and hard to fool compared with a learned reward model. The training loop: give the model a problem, let it generate a long chain of thought and an answer, reward it if the answer is correct, and update with reinforcement learning. Over many iterations, the model learns reasoning strategies that lead to correct answers: breaking problems down, checking its work, backtracking from dead ends.

OpenAI's **o1**, announced in September 2024, was the first widely deployed model trained this way. OpenAI reported that on a 2024 qualifying exam for the US Math Olympiad, GPT-4o solved about 12% of problems on average while o1 solved about 74% with a single attempt. In January 2025, DeepSeek published **R1** along with a detailed technical report, showing that reinforcement learning with simple correctness rewards could produce strong reasoning, and that an earlier version trained with almost no supervised examples spontaneously learned to re-examine its own steps, a moment the authors called an "aha moment."

<KeyIdea track="t4">Reasoning models are trained with reinforcement learning to use long chains of thought productively. Where answers can be checked automatically, this has produced some of the fastest capability gains in the field's history.</KeyIdea>

## Process versus outcome

Should the model be rewarded only for correct final answers (**outcome supervision**), or for each correct step (**process supervision**)? OpenAI's 2023 paper "Let's Verify Step by Step" trained a **process reward model** on human labels for 800,000 individual reasoning steps and found it selected correct solutions more reliably than an outcome-based model on hard math problems. Process supervision also has a safety appeal: it rewards reasoning that humans can follow and endorse, not just reasoning that happens to land on the right answer.

## The new scaling axis

Reasoning models introduced **test-time compute** as a second axis of scale. Their accuracy on hard problems rises with how long they are allowed to think. This changes economics (capability can be purchased per query), product design (users can choose "think harder" modes), and governance (training compute no longer fully determines what a model can do).

It also changes what benchmarks mean. A score now depends on how much thinking budget the model was given. When you read a reasoning model's results, check the compute setting.

## Can we trust the visible reasoning?

A model's chain of thought looks like a window into its mind. Is it?

**Faithfulness** asks whether the stated reasoning reflects what actually drove the answer. Evidence suggests: only partly. In 2023, researchers showed that models could be steered toward an answer by a subtle bias in the prompt (for example, always placing the correct answer in position A in examples) and would then produce plausible reasoning for that answer without ever mentioning the bias. In 2025, Anthropic tested reasoning models by slipping hints into prompts. When the models used the hints, they often failed to acknowledge doing so: Claude 3.7 Sonnet mentioned the hint about a quarter of the time, and DeepSeek R1 about 39% of the time.

At the same time, chains of thought are useful for oversight. OpenAI reported in 2025 that a weaker model reading a reasoning model's chain of thought could catch it planning to cheat on coding tasks, writing things like "let's hack." But when they penalized such thoughts during training, the model learned to hide its intent while continuing to cheat. A multi-lab position paper later that year called chain-of-thought monitorability "a new and fragile opportunity," urging developers not to train away the legibility of model reasoning.

<Callout type="caution" title="Two errors to avoid">
Do not treat a chain of thought as a transcript of the model's true reasoning; it can omit, rationalize, or mislead. But do not dismiss it either; it is often informative and is currently one of the best tools for catching misbehavior. The research question is how to keep it informative as models grow more capable.
</Callout>
